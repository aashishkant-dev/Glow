Modern hospital stays are shorter than ever. Procedures that once meant a week of recovery in a ward now result in discharge within 24–48 hours. For patients and families, that efficiency is a double-edged sword.

Going home quickly is good — familiar environments support healing. But going home *without adequate support* is where complications happen. A PSW in the days and weeks following surgery can be the difference between smooth recovery and a return to emergency.

## What happens at hospital discharge

When a patient is cleared to go home, the hospital's discharge planning team assembles a care plan. This plan often includes:

- Follow-up appointments with a surgeon or GP
- A referral to Ontario Health atHome for nursing visits (if clinically required)
- Prescription medications with specific instructions
- Mobility restrictions and activity limitations
- Dietary guidance

What the discharge plan often *doesn't* include: who is going to prepare meals, help the patient bathe, manage the household, and ensure they don't trip on the way to the bathroom at 2am.

That gap is where families scramble — and where a PSW fills a critical role.

## What a PSW does during post-surgical recovery

PSWs are not nurses and do not perform clinical procedures like changing surgical dressings. But the non-clinical support they provide is essential:

- **Mobility assistance** — helping patients move safely between bed, chair, toilet, and shower; preventing falls during the most vulnerable period
- **Bathing and personal care** — assisting with sponge baths or showers when a patient cannot safely manage alone post-surgery
- **Meal preparation** — cooking and serving meals that align with post-surgical dietary requirements; ensuring adequate hydration
- **Medication reminders** — prompting patients to take prescribed medications on schedule; flagging missed doses to family
- **Light housekeeping** — maintaining a safe, sanitary environment during recovery
- **Companionship and monitoring** — keeping the patient company, watching for signs of distress, and contacting family if something seems wrong

For elderly patients especially, this kind of wraparound support is what keeps a short recovery from becoming a longer crisis.
